# Process: Detailed Instructions and Examples

This file contains the step-by-step guidance, examples, and verification checklists for using book-compiler.

---

## Pass 1: Survey — Detailed Instructions

**Goal**: Establish orientation. Understand the book's shape before diving into reading.

### Step 1.1: Identify the Text Type

**Ask yourself:**
- Is this primarily non-fiction (exposition, argument, methodology) or narrative (story, memoir)?
- Is it structured as a single argument, a collection of essays, reference material, or exploratory writing?
- What is the author's primary mode: convincing, explaining, teaching, exploring, provoking?

**Why it matters**: Different text types demand different reading strategies.

### Step 1.2: Skim the Structure

**Do not read linearly yet.** Instead:

1. Read the title, subtitle, front matter (dedication, preface)
2. Read the table of contents or chapter titles
3. Read the introduction (if present)—this is often where the author states the problem and intent
4. Read the conclusion or epilogue—this is where the author states the implications
5. Sample 2-3 chapter openings—just the first 1-2 paragraphs
6. Glance at back matter (index, bibliography, notes)

**Time**: Should take 10-20 minutes for a book, 5 minutes for an article.

### Step 1.3: Identify the Problem

**Ask**: What difficulty, gap, or challenge prompted the author to write this?

This is not always stated explicitly. You may need to infer it from:
- The introduction ("This book addresses the misunderstanding that...")
- The title or subtitle
- The author's preface or opening argument

**Write it down** in 1-2 sentences. Example:
- Problem: "Readers typically approach all books the same way, leading to shallow understanding of complex texts."

### Step 1.4: Identify the Intent

**Ask**: What does the author aim to accomplish?

- To convince you of something?
- To teach you a method?
- To challenge your assumptions?
- To provide reference material?
- To explore a question?

Intent and problem are related but not identical. A problem is what's wrong; intent is what the author wants to do about it.

**Example**:
- Problem: "Shallow reading leaves readers unprepared to think critically about what they read."
- Intent: "Teach readers a systematic methodology for deep reading that preserves the author's intellectual structure while enabling critical evaluation."

### Step 1.5: Identify Central Questions

**Ask**: What question(s) does the author set out to answer?

These are often stated in the introduction or implied by the structure:
- "How should one read a difficult philosophy text?"
- "What is the relationship between method and understanding?"
- "Can a reader truly reconstruct an author's thought?"

**Write** the 1-3 central questions. These become Question nodes later.

### Step 1.6: Determine Your Reading Goal

Before you commit to reading, clarify:
- **Depth**: Do you need the full reconstruction, or a strategic overview?
- **Scope**: Will you read the entire text or specific sections?
- **Time budget**: How much time can you invest?

**Output of Pass 1**:
- One-paragraph orientation (what this book is, who wrote it, what it addresses)
- 1-2 sentences on the problem and author's intent
- 1-3 central questions listed
- A sense of the book's structure and scope

## Pass 2: Reconstruct — Detailed Instructions

**Goal**: Read the text and extract the Five-Layer Model.

**Critical rule**: Never critique before reconstruction is complete. You are not evaluating the author's argument. You are understanding it on its own terms.

### Step 2.1: Read Actively

As you read the text (linearly or by sections):
- Mark passages that introduce major concepts
- Mark passages where the author makes important claims
- Mark passages where the author defends a claim with evidence or example
- Mark transitions where the author moves from one idea to the next

You are not taking notes yet. You are marking the structure.

### Step 2.2: Extract Nodes as You Read

As you encounter each major intellectual move, ask:

**When you see a term or definition:**
- Is this a concept the author is introducing as a building block?
- Is it used throughout the text?
- Is it necessary for understanding other ideas?
- → If yes: Create a **Concept** node.

**When you see a claim or assertion:**
- Is the author asserting this as true?
- Is it central to the book's thesis or a sub-point?
- Does it connect to the book's central questions?
- → If yes: Create a **Claim** node.

**When you see a general rule or principle:**
- Is this a law or principle the author is establishing?
- Does it apply to multiple situations?
- Is it a major takeaway?
- → If yes: Create a **Principle** node.

**When you see evidence:**
- Does this data, observation, or quotation support a specific claim?
- Is it authoritative and traceable?
- → Create an **Evidence** node and link it with SUPPORTS.

**When you see an example or illustration:**
- Is this a concrete instance of a general idea?
- Is it illustrative (helps explain) or constitutive (is itself the idea)?
- → Create an **Example** node with ILLUSTRATES relation (not a Concept node).

**When you see an argument:**
- Does the author link premises to a conclusion?
- Does it defend a claim?
- → Create an **Argument** node and link it with SUPPORTS.

### Step 2.3: Node Admission Rule

**A node earns a place if:**
- It is necessary for understanding the central ideas
- It is a dependency of other important nodes
- It represents a significant original contribution by the author
- It is required for application or use of the ideas
- Losing it would distort the author's position

**Drop a node if:**
- It is purely illustrative (use ILLUSTRATES relation instead)
- It is a passing mention not connected to the core argument
- It is a sub-point of a supporting idea (fold into the parent)
- It is redundant with another node

**Rule of thumb**: If a node doesn't support the book's central questions or serves the author's central intent, question whether it belongs.

### Step 2.4: Tag Every Node with Metadata

For every node, record:

**Status** (epistemic tag):
- **explicit** — the author states it directly ("Reading should be purposeful")
- **inferred** — clearly implied but not stated verbatim (the author discusses reading goals and speed; you infer "purpose determines method")
- **interpretation** — a reading of ambiguous passages (requires your judgment)
- **evaluation** — your (or the skill's) assessment; not something the author claimed

**Importance**:
- **core** — central to the thesis; removing it breaks the argument
- **important** — significant; supports core ideas; needed for deep understanding
- **supporting** — reinforces important ideas; often an example, detail, sub-argument
- **detail** — illustrative or ancillary; included for completeness but not essential

**Confidence**:
- **high** — clear, unambiguous, well-supported in the text
- **medium** — reasonable interpretation but not explicit; some ambiguity
- **low** — inferred or interpretive; depends on reading assumptions

**Source** (traceability):
- Chapter: Chapter number or section name
- Location: Page range, passage, or paragraph description
- Example: "Chapter 1: The Importance of Purpose", "pp. 12-15, opening argument"

### Step 2.5: Identify Relations

As you build the model, trace how nodes connect:

| Relation | Use When |
|----------|----------|
| **ANSWERS** | A Claim or Idea answers a Question |
| **SUPPORTS** | Evidence or Argument supports a Claim |
| **DEPENDS_ON** | One Idea depends on another Concept or Principle |
| **EXPLAINS** | A Concept explains or defines another Concept |
| **ILLUSTRATES** | An Example illustrates a Concept or Claim |
| **QUALIFIES** | A Limitation qualifies a Claim (scope, exception) |
| **CONTRADICTS** | One Claim contradicts another (flag tensions in the text) |
| **LEADS_TO** | An Implication leads to a Consequence or Application |
| **PART_OF** | An Idea is a component of a larger framework |

**Do not assume relations.** Only draw relations that are explicitly or clearly implied in the text.

### Step 2.6: Preserve Qualifications

**Critical**: Do not lose nuance in the compression phase.

If the author says: "In most cases, X leads to Y, except when Z is present,"
- Do NOT compress to: "X leads to Y"
- Do PRESERVE as a Claim node with a Limitation node that qualifies it

If the author says: "Perhaps the most important factor is...",
- Do NOT present as unqualified: "The most important factor is..."
- Do PRESERVE the uncertainty in the statement or tag confidence as "medium"

**Output of Pass 2**:
A collection of ~30-100 nodes (depending on text length and depth), organized by layer, each with full metadata and relations.

## Pass 3: Write — Detailed Instructions

**Goal**: Render the reconstructed model as five structured markdown files.

### Step 3.1: Organize Nodes by Layer

Group all nodes into their layer:
- PURPOSE → 00_purpose.md
- QUESTIONS → 01_questions.md
- IDEAS → 02_ideas.md
- REASONING → 03_reasoning.md
- CONSEQUENCES → 04_consequences.md

### Step 3.2: Structure Each File Hierarchically

Within each file:
1. Start with a brief section header (1-2 sentences explaining the layer's role in the book)
2. Group nodes by theme or sub-topic (use markdown headings `##`, `###`)
3. Present nodes in logical order (usually top-down: purpose → questions → ideas answering them → reasoning supporting ideas → consequences flowing from them)
4. End with a reflection on how this layer functions in the book

**Example structure for 02_ideas.md:**

```
## IDEAS: Concepts, Claims, and Principles

The book introduces X key concepts that form the foundation of its argument...

### Foundational Concepts

#### Concept: Deep Reading
[node details]

#### Concept: Intellectual Structure
[node details]

### Core Claims

#### Claim: Reading is Purposeful
[node details]

### Principles

#### Principle: Reconstruction Before Compression
[node details]
```

### Step 3.3: Format Each Node

Use this template:

```markdown
#### [Type]: [Title]

**Statement:** [1-2 sentence statement of the idea or claim]

- **Type:** [Concept | Claim | Principle | Argument | Evidence | Example | Assumption | Implication | Application | Limitation]
- **Status:** [explicit | inferred | interpretation | evaluation]
- **Importance:** [core | important | supporting | detail]
- **Confidence:** [high | medium | low]
- **Source:** [Chapter X, section "..." or "pp. 12-15, opening argument"]

**Relations:**
- [RELATION TYPE] → [target node] — [brief note explaining the connection]
- [RELATION TYPE] → [target node] — [brief note]

**[Optional] Elaboration:**
[1 paragraph of context or nuance if the statement alone isn't sufficient]
```

### Step 3.4: Write Relations Thoughtfully

Relations are not decoration. They show how the book's ideas fit together.

**Good**: "SUPPORTS claim C-042: The evidence from the 1985 study demonstrates that readers who establish purpose first retain material 40% better, directly supporting the claim that purpose precedes processing."

**Bad**: "SUPPORTS C-042"

Always explain the connection.

### Step 3.5: Cross-File Links

If a node in one file relates to a node in another:
- Add a cross-reference: "See Evidence E-015 in 03_reasoning.md for experimental support."
- This helps the reader navigate the model.

## Example: How to Handle a Passage

**Passage** (hypothetical):

> "Reading is purposeful. You cannot read everything at the same pace or depth. Before you open a book, you must ask yourself: Why am I reading this? The answer determines how you read. A mystery novel demands linear attention; a reference book demands selective attention; a philosophy text demands critical attention. Purpose determines method."

### Node Extraction

```yaml
---
id: Q-001
type: Question
title: Why am I reading this book?

statement: |
  A foundational question that must precede reading. The answer determines 
  reading strategy and depth.

status: explicit
importance: core
confidence: high

source:
  chapter: 1
  location: "Introduction, paragraph 3"

relations: []

---

id: P-001
type: Principle
title: Purpose Determines Method

statement: |
  The reason for reading (the reading goal) determines the approach, pace, 
  and depth of reading. Different text types require different reading methods.

status: explicit
importance: core
confidence: high

source:
  chapter: 1
  location: "Introduction, final sentence of opening argument"

relations:
  - type: ANSWERS
    target: Q-001
    note: "Provides the answer to the foundational question of why reading matters"
  - type: DEPENDS_ON
    target: C-001
    note: "Depends on the concept of 'purpose' being established as distinct and deliberate"

---

id: E-001
type: Example
title: Different Text Types Require Different Methods

statement: |
  A mystery novel demands linear attention to follow the plot. A reference 
  book demands selective, targeted attention. A philosophy text demands 
  critical, evaluative attention.

status: explicit
importance: supporting
confidence: high

source:
  chapter: 1
  location: "Introduction, middle of opening argument"

relations:
  - type: ILLUSTRATES
    target: P-001
    note: "Illustrates the principle that purpose determines method with three concrete examples"
```

### What This Shows

- The **idea** ("Purpose Determines Method") is a Principle node — a rule the author establishes
- The **question** ("Why am I reading?") is its own Question node — it organizes inquiry
- The **examples** (mystery, reference, philosophy) are separated into an Example node with an ILLUSTRATES relation, NOT folded into the principle
- **Status, importance, and confidence** are explicit and honest
- **Source** is precise (not just "chapter 1" but a location within the introduction)
- **Relations** show how nodes connect

## Verification Checklist

Before considering a book "read," verify:

- ✓ Purpose and Intent nodes are explicit and sourced
- ✓ Central Questions are identified as distinct Question nodes
- ✓ Ideas are separated into Concepts, Claims, Principles (not mixed)
- ✓ Every important Claim has Evidence or Argument supporting it (SUPPORTS relation)
- ✓ Examples are separate nodes with ILLUSTRATES relations, not merged into ideas
- ✓ All nodes have status tags (explicit/inferred/interpretation/evaluation)
- ✓ No inferred statements presented as explicit (status tags are honest)
- ✓ Every important node has a source (chapter/section/location)
- ✓ Relations are meaningful and labeled with explanatory notes
- ✓ Qualifications, scope limitations, and exceptions are preserved in statements or as Limitation nodes
- ✓ No node tries to say five things (if a node is too big, split it)
- ✓ The five files tell a coherent story (reading PURPOSE → QUESTIONS → IDEAS → REASONING → CONSEQUENCES shows how the book builds)
- ✓ No critique in the nodes (evaluation nodes are explicitly tagged as such, not mixed with author's explicit claims)

## Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

**Pitfall 1: Treating examples as ideas**
- ❌ Create a Concept node "The sun rises" based on the example "The sun rises at my house every morning"
- ✓ Create a Concept node "The sun rises in the east" and an Example node for the house illustration with ILLUSTRATES

**Pitfall 2: Losing qualifications**
- ❌ Extract: "X causes Y"
- ✓ Extract: "X usually causes Y, except in circumstances Z" (preserve scope as written or as a Limitation node)

**Pitfall 3: Presenting inferences as explicit statements**
- ❌ Tag as `status: explicit` when the author implies but doesn't state it
- ✓ Tag as `status: inferred` and note in the statement that it's inferred

**Pitfall 4: Skipping source traceability**
- ❌ Create a node with no source
- ✓ Every node knows where it came from (chapter, section, page)

**Pitfall 5: Not preserving nuance in compression**
- ❌ Compress "Perhaps the most important factor in most cases is..." to "The most important factor is..."
- ✓ Keep "perhaps" and "most cases" in the statement; tag confidence as "medium"

**Pitfall 6: Creating nodes that are too big**
- ❌ One node with statement: "The book argues that purpose is important, method depends on purpose, and reading types vary."
- ✓ Three nodes: Q-001 (question), P-001 (principle), E-001 (example)

## FAQ

**Q: How many nodes should a book have?**
A: Depends on book length and depth. A ~300-page non-fiction book typically yields 40-80 nodes. A ~50-page article, 10-20. There's no hard target.

**Q: What if the author contradicts themselves?**
A: Create both nodes. Use a CONTRADICTS relation. Tag status appropriately. This is valuable information about the text.

**Q: What if the author's intent seems different from what they explicitly state?**
A: Create nodes for both. Use an inferred status for the intent-node. Let the reader decide.

**Q: Should I include every detail or just major ideas?**
A: Use the Node Admission Rule. If removing it would not distort the author's position, it's optional. Include it if it helps the reader understand.

**Q: How long should the statement be?**
A: 1-2 sentences max. If you need more, that goes in the optional "Elaboration" section, not in the statement.

**Q: Should I reorganize the book's structure in the output?**
A: No. Respect the order and structure the author chose. Your job is to make the structure visible, not to reorganize it.
